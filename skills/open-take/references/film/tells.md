## What makes a film look machine-made

A viewer who has seen a few films made by agents starts to recognise them —
not by any one flaw, but by choices that come back from film to film,
whatever the product. Below are the ones that came back most: each was found
in a large share of 133 agent-made product films across 18 products, and is
rare or done differently in launch films people shared. Each is described so
it can be pointed at, at a second of a film.

None of them is wrong in itself. A pattern is a clue, not a rule: each entry
says when the same choice is fine, and a film can have one for its own good
reason. What gives a film away is the default — the choice made because it
was the first one to hand, not because this product called for it.

### Words

**A perks line under the logo.** In the last seconds, under the mark and the
URL, a small line of two to four perks joined by dots: "Free · No sign-up ·
Open source", "No account. No install. It just opens." The same line closed
films of very different products. Shared launch films end on the mark, one
sentence and where to go; a licence or price fact, when it appears, appears
alone and specific.
*Fine when* one of those perks is what sets the product apart and the film
argued it — "works offline" after a shot of nothing being uploaded. Then say
that one thing, once.

**A question to the viewer before the product.** The first two to six
seconds are a sentence or two on a plain ground, addressed to "you": a
rhetorical question ("Ever lost track of…?") or a small pain ("Your API sent
you this."). The product appears after it has been read. Shared launch films
put something moving and specific on screen in the first fraction of a
second; when they open on words, the words are kinetic and carry the
product's own objects, and a question is rare.
*Fine when* the question is the product's own verb and is asked inside the
product — typed into it, not on a card before it — or when a pain is a small
story the product resolves in the same frame.

**The first hook that comes to mind.** Agents given the same product,
separately, opened on the same premise and often the same line — the same
"got an idea?", the same before/after, the same three boxes labelled
Idea → Sketch → Ship. The first idea for a product is most people's first
idea. Launch films of one product, made with the same material, opened in as
many ways as there were films.
*Fine when* it is still the best idea after you have had others.

**Subtitles that narrate the click.** A caption on screen during nearly every
action, saying what the picture already shows: "Drag the box — the arrow
follows." while the box is dragged and the arrow follows. Shared launch films
carry the idea in their words ("Draw what you mean"), each line on its own,
three to six lines in a short film — never a running commentary.
*Fine when* the action cannot be read without help — a shortcut, a hidden
state — and one line for the idea of a sequence, not one per click.

**Captions about the operation, in the product's own terms.** The line says
what was done and names it the way the interface does — "One toggle, 12,000
rows become a pivot", "auto-grouped into clusters", "nodes", "layers" —
while the toggle is a few pixels wide and the viewer has never seen the
panel it sits in. Someone new reads the words and cannot tell what they are
looking at. Shared launch films name what is on screen in everyday words
and why it is worth a look ("every dot is one order, the day it shipped"),
and leave the mechanism to the picture.
*Fine when* the control is shown large enough to read and doing it is the
point — then its name is what the viewer just watched.

**A caption the picture does not show.** The words promise something the
frame does not deliver: "in 3D" over a model seen straight on, where it
reads as flat; "replayed" over a picture that hardly moves;
"automatically" over a result already there when the shot opens. A viewer
reads the claim, looks for it, and stops trusting the next one.
*Fine when* the picture is changed until it shows it — turn it until the
depth shows, start the shot before the result appears — or the words
change to what it does show.

**Slogans from a mould.** Captions built from the same few shapes: "Do X. Get
Y." ("Paste code. Get a picture."), "Same X. New Y.", "Think it, and draw
it." Used as the grammar of every caption they read as generated. Shared
launch films' lines are single claims or images, often with a product object
set inside the sentence.
*Fine* once, as the tagline.

**Badges and fine print that ride along.** Labels that stay on screen —
"demo data", "real recording · content is a demo", "SAMPLE" — dated source
lines, legal hedges. Shared launch films carry none of these as badges; the
one sourced number a film leans on gets its date as part of the design.
Where something on screen is made up, there are quieter ways to let it show:
content that is plainly a sketch or an example by what it is, a line said
once where it matters, fine print once at the end.
*Fine when* one dated source sits under the one number the film rests on, or
required fine print appears once, small.

### Structure

**A tour.** After the hook, a card per thing the product does or claims, in
the order of its page or a user's clicks, often numbered ("01 Paste… 02 Pick…
03 Export"), with nothing in one card still there in the next, even where a
seam flies a shape across (`skill film story`). Shared launch films about
features still tell one idea through the product's objects — many notes,
one click, every note answered — setup, proof, payoff, in a few seconds each.
*Fine when* the film is a how-to.

**The logo twice.** A title card with the logo and a tagline in the first
seconds, then the same lockup at the end. Shared launch films hold the title
until something has happened; the logo is almost always the last thing.
*Fine when* the mark is the news, or as a small mark in a corner.

**Ending more than once.** A card of claims or a ticked list ("✓ Free ✓ Works
offline ✓ Autosaves"), then a second pitch, then the logo. The films people
shared landed each claim where it was shown and ended once; a run of
closing cards was the failure named among them.
*Fine when* the list is the product (a checklist, a changelog), or the film
opened by promising that list.

**Scenes swapped, nothing carried.** Every scene change a hard cut or a short
crossfade between flat screens and cards; nothing in one scene becomes the
next. Shared launch films leave a scene through something in it (`skill film
craft`: getting from one scene to the next) and keep hard cuts for
punctuation.
*Fine* as rhythm — a burst of cuts on the beat — when it is not the only way
scenes change.
*In the kit:* say on each segment what crosses and name it with `carry`;
`film render` says boundary by boundary whether it was carried, and draws a
strip of them (`skill film`, step 4).

**The same carry at every boundary.** Each scene leaves the same way — the
thing pressed grows until it fills the frame and becomes the next scene's
ground, three or four times in half a minute. Once, it reads as an idea; at
every boundary, as the film's only move. Shared launch films changed how
they left a scene from one boundary to the next, and kept one move that only
their product could make for the turn or the end.
*Fine when* it is the concept: one element carried through the whole film.

**Fade to black after the logo.** The lockup builds, holds, and the picture
fades out while the chord rings. Shared launch films hold the lockup still,
with one small motion, and end.

### Picture

**The screen laid flat.** The product shown as a flat recording or screenshot
filling the frame, the camera panning and zooming across it, scene after
scene; nothing leaves the screen, nothing turns in depth. Shared launch
films showed the whole screen once as proof, then staged it — tilted into
depth with its content still moving, its parts lifted off and moved as
objects, the camera pushing in on the part in use (`skill film craft`: the
product on screen).
*Fine when* the screen itself is the spectacle — a map or a canvas filling
with the product's own drawing — and the camera moves through it rather than
across it.
*In the kit:* `stage.adopt(el)` tilts a picture, a player or a part the page
already has, where it is; `explode` lifts a screen apart into its parts
(kit/stage.js).

**A screenshot as a thin sheet.** A whole page's screenshot placed in the
frame — floating or tilted — cut flush at its edges: no margin, no corner,
no shadow, text sliced at the border. It reads as a sheet of paper, not as
the product. Shared launch films showed a screen as an object with the
product's own surface — its cards' corners and shadow, a margin of its own
ground — or cropped to the part in use.
*Fine* for the frames of a push that ends inside the screen.
*In the kit:* `frame(look)` (kit/look.js) gives the product's own corner,
shadow and ground from `film look`, applied only where called.

**Boxes around the UI.** A coloured rectangle or ring snaps around the
element being talked about, often with the rest dimmed; push in, box, pull
out, next box. Shared launch films crop to the control, or draw one
hand-made mark (`skill film craft`: the product on screen).
*Fine when* it is the product's own highlight — a search hit, a match
colour — shown instead of an added one.

**Keycaps for every shortcut.** A keycap graphic pops up in a corner on each
key pressed. Shared launch films show the UI reacting.
*Fine when* the keyboard is the product's idea — then one key, large, once.

**The filmic stack.** Grain, vignette, letterbox bars, a stock photo and a
handheld drift, all at once for the whole film — what a word like
"cinematic" turns into by default. Shared launch films use one texture, and
only when it is the product's world (paper grain on a product about paper).
*Fine when* the material calls for one of them.

### What it sounds like

These came back in nearly every agent-made film; the launch films studied
were mostly silent, so they are less sure.

**One bed for every product.** A synth pad and plucked arpeggio over a
four-chord loop at 96–120 BPM, whatever the product.

**A sound on every event.** A tick on every key and click, a chime on every
result, a whoosh on every move — the picture's every change answered.

**The same sign-off.** Drums drop out, one long chord, a bell on the logo,
fade. *Fine* where the drums leave on the story's last turn and the chord
lands on the mark: the launch film measured in `reference/` ends this way,
and so do the films a viewer preferred (`START.md`).

**Notes between the notes.** A register whose pitched sound sits more than
15 cents off the piece's own tuning for more than 30% of its time: a bass
bent down through every note as a pun on the product's name, a bell built
from ratios that are not the scale's, a glide on each note. Every note is
written right and the whole still sounds out of tune. It came back in 21 of
145 agent-made films (`film listen`, "Pitch"). Fine for a single gesture —
a riser, a siren, a slide into the drop — not as the instrument.
