# Start here

`film init` wrote this folder. `skill film` says how the page is written,
rendered and checked. This page says what the films made with this tool
were liked and disliked for, what seven launch films people shared were
measured doing, and an order to work in. Read it, fill `PLAN.md`, read one
reference (step 3 below) and build a first draft; everything else here is
for looking things up while you build.

## What this page rests on

- **Fourteen films of four products made with this tool, watched by one
  viewer**, the last of them beside the product's own launch film, and
  after them two more of that product set beside it. What those viewings
  showed is below. It is the strongest evidence on this page, and it is one
  person's judgment.
- **Seven launch films measured frame by frame.** One was judged a very
  good launch in those viewings (`reference/`). Six others, by one maker of
  generative-video tools, posted to social video (`reference/travel/`);
  three of them were picked out for their camera, cutting, animation and
  music. No film
  made their way with this tool has been set beside ours. 91 more launch
  films, measured with one detector, give the spread.
- **What was never tested.** No film made with the moves, words and sound
  written from the six (`lib/moves.js`, `lib/hud.js`, `lib/space.js`,
  `lib/palette.mjs`) has been watched beside the preferred films — one was
  watched beside two others of its own product, and was not preferred to
  them (below); nor with
  `lib/devices.js`, `lib/handoff.js`, `lib/swarm.js`, `lib/physics.js` or
  `lib/solid.js`,
  written after the last film below, which none of the liked films had. And
  no number measured on our films sorted the preferred ones from the rest:
  one preferred film never cut and held still 53% of the time; one judged
  not good-looking cut 8.6 times a minute and held 69%; a film made with
  another tool from the same brief, which was picked over one of ours and a
  third tool's in a blind viewing (the first of the three preferred films
  beat it later), cut 10 times a minute and held 68%. The numbers
  below say what films did, not what will be liked.

## What viewings showed

Six were made from a one-line request and a guide of advice. They were
judged middling, rigid and not good-looking, with nothing standing out.
They were tours — the product's picture, a claim, a feature, another feature, a line
on trust, the logo — each a card of its own, nothing carried from one card
into the next; the product as screenshots, pushed in on or tilted, soft once
enlarged; a caption or a big number on nearly every card; a camera that
never stopped drifting or pushing in; a hit on every cut and every number,
over an oscillator score; a logo card held three to six seconds.

Three were preferred to each of those: judged not rigid, their information
clear, their transitions and animation good-looking. The three look
nothing like each other — a slow web page and the photo that slows it; a
photo as a slab whose thickness is its file size; a membership card handed
to the next person. What they shared:

- **one thing from the product's world, followed from the first frame to
  the last**; the last view is the first view, changed;
- **the thing in trouble, with a number on it, and the film ending on that
  number changed**: a 2.79 MB photo that a page waits ten seconds for, at
  the end 276 kB and the page there in one; ten months left on a contract
  and NT$2,576 to leave it, at the end NT$300 to hand it on;
- **three to five of the product's functions happening to that thing**,
  none of them as a card — most of what those small products do;
- **material at the size where the trouble is real**: the product's own
  sample, because it was 2.79 MB; where a product's own is too small to be
  anyone's problem, public data fetched without an account, or one written
  for the film and marked as a demo where it stands for someone's data;
- **more than one place**: a white web page, a studio floor, a cream table,
  and the product's interface arriving into them in its own colours;
- **the interface rebuilt as live HTML** in its own fonts, colours, wording
  and formulas, the cursor working it; a screenshot only as evidence;
- **every change of scene something on screen doing something**: a card
  flips over into the next view; a blob swallows the photo and opens as the
  editor; the page is squashed into the logo;
- built the way the reference film is built: the camera at rest much of the
  time while things are made one at a time; three to six lines of caption;
  recorded instruments, only the story's turns on bar lines, drums in at the
  result, clicks and typing silent, a handful of sounds each on an arrival;
- 37.6, 30.5 and 38.5 s: what those products came to, not a length to aim
  for.

The story was never given as a reason. The reasons given were the motion
and the transitions, the information being clear, the film not being rigid —
and, of the films after, how much was in them.

Two were made from an earlier copy of this page. Both followed one thing
and rebuilt the interface live. One was placed just under the three. The
other was judged to have little in it. It put the sample the product opens
with through its main function, retyped one value and folded it into the
logo; its plan listed seven things the product does and the film showed
one; nothing was wrong at the start, so nothing was better at the end; its
first-time viewer said the sample made it look like a toy, and the note was
turned down because a larger one "would have to be invented"; its first
draft was 29 s and every round took time out, to 21.6 s. That product's film
was made twice more with real public data, five and six of its functions
happening to it and a number that changed. Both were judged fine. Still weak
in both: one dark colour from end to end, and the product pulled back to
blocks too small to read.

The one of the six judged not good-looking had copied the measured
surface of another tool's film — bigger type, more tweens, more hits. What
the viewing found against it: a film does not have to keep zooming in.

The last was for a product that does more: its plan listed thirteen things.
It followed one thing, opened on trouble with a number on it and rebuilt
the interface live. Watched beside the product's own launch film, it was
judged the weaker: the product's film much more complete, ours too short,
and the product's 3D effects and transitions better than ours. What in its
making led there:

- six of the thirteen were in; the rest were left out in one line that
  named them, with no reason given for any;
- it was planned at 39 s, inside the 30 to 45 this page then gave; its
  drafts ran 40, 34.5 and 31 s, both rounds of first-time viewers answered
  by cutting; a stretch from 15 to 28 s where only a counter moved was
  shortened, not given one of the functions left out;
- six stretches in 31 s; the product's own film runs 57.7 s in about
  thirteen, a different function in each;
- its laptop was four lines of CSS perspective, and shut, a flat trapezoid;
  its phone and computers were flat outlines. In the product's film a lit
  laptop stands large in three-quarter view and shuts its lid in about
  0.3 s, a phone rises from below, the computers are rounded lit boxes with
  a status light, and a button the cursor clicks turns into one of them;
- it changed scene five times, mostly by pulling back or pushing in. The
  product's film changes about thirteen times, each in 0.4 to 0.5 s and
  none a hard cut: the scene whips out while the bot's avatar stays on
  screen and travels to its next place; a panel rises from below; a click
  on a row pushes through into its detail; the laptop comes up from below
  as the scene before it leaves upward.

What that comparison adds: how long a film is and how much of the product
it shows follow from how much the product does, and the film judged
better moved more between its scenes and had depth that read as objects.

Two more were then made of that product and the three set side by side:
the one above (held 59% of its length), one from this folder with the camera
at rest while things are made (63%), and one made to keep the camera
travelling (19.6%), the product's avatar carried through twelve places. The
travelling one was felt to hurry for its own sake — the avatar moving
about — and no clear preference emerged between the three. What that adds:
a smaller held share is not by itself a gain, and
a thing carried needs a reason each time it moves. Both ways stay below,
side by side; neither is the default.

The films to beat are the tour, the thin one and the short one.

## What the measured films do

Seven launch films: the reference and the six. Each item says what to do,
with their numbers, the call that does it, and what it looks like done
badly. Where they differ, both ways are given with what each rests on:
choose by the product.

**The picture seldom holds.** In the six, an event detector calls 11–19%
of the length a hold (91 launch films: median 40%), and the camera or the
whole picture travels 47–82% of the time. The reference holds its camera
still in 48.5% of frames, yet its picture is unchanged in only about 10%:
panels, strokes, rows and numbers arrive one at a time under the still
camera, the longest dead stretch 0.4–0.7 s. Our three preferred films held
26–53% by the same detector, re-encoded to the six's size and frame rate
(that detector is not part of open-take). So two ways keep a picture alive:
a camera that travels from station to station, which rests on the six,
three of them films picked out in the viewings; or a camera at rest while
the picture is made in front of it, which rests on the one film judged very
good and the three of ours that were preferred. Made both ways for one
product, 19.6% held against 63%, neither was preferred. Where
the six hold, it is to read or count, once dead still on a failure, once to
see a whole interface (0.58 s), and something still moves inside each hold.
A tour of stations in one take accelerates for 5–6 frames, slows for 10–12
and dwells 3–8 without reaching zero. `stations`, `exitDrift`
(`lib/moves.js`); `cam.frame` (`kit/camera.js`); `film study film.mp4`
reads a film's holds and travel on the scale of its own figures for the six
(holds 4–17%, travel 35–50%) and for 67 catalogue films (42%, 17%).
*Done badly:* every hold pushed in on (a film does not have to keep
zooming in); a camera drifting with nothing being made; nine seconds where only a
counter moves; travel to bring the held share down, the carried thing sent
to a new place at every boundary with nothing taking it there (hurry for
its own sake). A drift meant to keep a hold alive may be too faint to
register: sparse dots drifting changed 1–1.6% of the pixels and `film
probe` still read a freeze, where a 3.5% push did not (whether an eye
tells the two apart was not tested; a drifting hold and a locked one were
not told apart when compared once).

**Layers move separately.** Most of the six's "3D" is flat layers scaled
apart: a window lifts toward the lens about 3× while the desk under it stays
at 0.99–1.04 and only slides; a status chip does not move through a
three-frame push; a stack is drawn back to front with the focus in its
middle (the back card 19% as sharp as the focal one, the front 44%). Real
perspective comes in a few shots: a tilted map, an object orbited, a
corridor flown through. The reference pans and zooms one plane, with one
fly-through of logos in depth. `windowHop`, `pullbackReveal`
(`lib/moves.js`); slabs in depth with `kit/stage.js`; `lib/devices.js`;
`lib/space.js` with `lib/solid.js` when the lens itself moves in space or
the focus falls off with distance.
*Done badly:* the desk scaled with the window (a page that did so measured
desk 1.09→1.80 and window 1.24×: one zoom, not a lift); a CSS stack shrunk
with `scale()`, which flattens it — push it away with `translateZ`; "near"
and "far" set in the empty space beside the subject instead of in front of
it and behind it; a laptop of four lines of perspective.

**A change of picture carries something across, or hides in motion.** The
six change picture 11–29 times a minute, mostly by designed moves of
0.05–0.5 s (59 such transitions in 13 launch films: median 0.25 s). In one,
something visibly crosses 16 of 27 changes; in another, 26 of 27 cuts have
something on both sides — the same light, blur, object or colour, or motion
in the same direction. Long distances inside one interface are not flown:
the push speeds up, jumps 1.45–4× in one frame and settles in 6–8. A
momentum cut: the outgoing shot accelerates, the cut sits on its fastest
frame, the incoming one carries on the same way and slows. The reference
hands over 19 times in 57.8 s, through objects, push-ins and whips; checked
frame by frame, three of those are hard cuts, each on the fastest frame of
a move, and `film probe` does not find them. `momentumCut`, `rampJump`, `zoomThrough`,
`toThumbnail`, `implodeTo`, `blurBridge` (`lib/moves.js`); `whip` with a
`carry`, `panel`, `through` (`lib/handoff.js`). Declare each cut a move
makes — `tl.event(m.cutAt, "cut")`, `"hidden-cut"` for one inside a move, a
lift's span as `handoff: "lift"` — and `film render` draws a strip of every
boundary.
*Done badly:* the same carry at every boundary; a push too fast to see what
it went into; a match cut a few pixels off, which reads as a jump; a hard
cut where neither side moves and nothing crosses, other than as the odd
punctuation (one of 27 in the film that counted; `film probe` names it);
one long push where a jump belonged (rebuilt both ways, the continuous one
measured blurred early, like a slow push rather than a snap into place); five scene
changes in 31 s where the product's own film made thirteen. Seams and
`scenes()` hide a scene with `visibility: hidden` and show it with
`inherit`: a child given `visibility: visible` shows through its hidden
scene, so inside a scene write `inherit` or nothing.

**Words pinned to the screen say what is happening.** The six keep words on
screen nearly all the time, fixed to the frame so the camera never moves
them: a status chip for each step, in the present tense, three or four
words led by an -ing verb, 0.03–0.05 of the frame's height, sliding across
a cut to change its text; a prompt typed fast then slow (about 43–59
characters a second, the newest warm and cooling in 4–8 frames); a title
that appears in 0.1–0.2 s and holds 0.8–2.5 s; 121–280 readable words a
film, 2.9–4.7 a second where counted; one line of call to action, 0.028–0.049
of the height, there from its first frame. The reference has about 30 words
of its own; our preferred films had three to six lines, and their
information was judged clear. Many words or few, what holds in both is that
each line says what is happening on screen now, or the idea — never the
operation's name over a control too small to see. A status chip is the
product's own status when it has one. `layer`, `chip`, `statusLine`,
`prompt`, `voice`, `agent`, `detect`, `title` (`lib/hud.js`); `film
probe`'s text lane says how big the words read on a phone.
*Done badly:* a caption narrating the click ("Drag the box — the arrow
follows"); the interface's own terms for something the viewer has never
seen; words riding with the camera, smeared by a push or carried past the
frame's edge; a caption the picture does not show; badges that ride along.

**The sound's structure turns on the reveals.** In six of the seven, cuts
and picture events land on the beat no more often than chance would put
them (the seventh's tempo is unsettled); in all seven the sound changes
where the story turns. The six run a continuous bed: energy under 60 Hz
only 4.5–10 dB below the total and there 71–90% of the time (the three
measured in detail), 6–11 onsets a second, no silence before the last
second, five of them at −5.4 to −8.5 LUFS and one at −14.1. The low end
comes in on a reveal (+9 to +28 dB) or drops out (−19 dB), a build drops on
the cut that reveals the result, a single hit marks the start or landing of
a camera move, and the loudest half-seconds are not the busiest. The
reference has no low end for 15.6 s, then kick and sub on the bar where the
answer starts to be drawn; the low pulse doubles for its third product;
drums leave after the closing line, the logo lands on the next bar, then
silence; a few pitched effects, typing and clicks silent; −15.5 LUFS. Our
earlier scores had their sub 14.6–21.4 dB below the total, there 10–38% of
the time, 3.5–3.7 onsets a second, at −16. How dense is the choice; the
turn on the reveal is common to all. The kit masters to −16 LUFS (`skill
film numbers` says why); a louder master is a choice to make on purpose. `bed`, `hit`, `hits`,
`build`, `drop` (`lib/palette.mjs`); `subIn`, `dropOut`, `filterOpen`,
`filterClose` (`kit/audio.mjs`); `node lib/palette.mjs score.wav` sets a
score beside the six's figures; `film listen` lays it against the cuts.
*Done badly:* a sound on every event (the tours: a hit on every cut and
every number); a whoosh on every move; cuts forced onto beats while the
reveal lands after the music has decayed; a hit that does not stand over
the bed (mix the bed first; `hit` stands 4.8 dB over what its bus holds).

**Asked, then made.** All six tell one shape: a title card in the first
0–5 s → a request typed into a prompt box (at 7–13% of the length) → sent,
shown on the button (a push-in, a press of 2–6 frames, a burst) → a status
line for each step → the thing built in stages, each more laborious → a
check or a choice (a yes or no, four variants with one outlined, a failure
with three running numbers) → the result out in the world, with one line of
call to action. 71 prompt-then-result launch films (median 40.8 s) put the
first result at 46% of the length, and 59 of them end on a brand section.
It fits a product that is asked for something and makes it. For a product
that changes one thing — a file made smaller, a contract handed on — the
shape of the three preferred films is the same arc: the thing in trouble
with a number, the product happening to it, the number changed. The six are
one maker's films, and their template may be that maker's house style.
*Done badly:* a result that fades in after a pointer travels to a button; a
request any product's film could carry; a status line narrating over a
picture that does not change.

## Held beside a film you admire

`node lib/measure.mjs reference.mp4 draft.mp4` puts both on one ruler: the
picture as the share of pixels changing each frame, the sound as level,
bands, texture and width. Seven launch films of the travelling kind, measured
this way:

| picture | the seven | films here that stopped between moves |
|---|---|---|
| frames standing still | 23–44% | 54–78% |
| moves a minute | 35–47 | 21–22 |
| a move builds over / falls over | 8–15 / 9–18 frames | 6–9 / 8–13 |
| gaps between moves where the picture comes to rest | 24–59% | 67–93% |
| cuts a minute | 6–22 | 0 |
| first move or cut | 0.1–3.2 s, half by 1.3 s | 1.2–2.6 s |

| sound | the seven | scores here written note by note |
|---|---|---|
| level swing, second to second | 2.4–12 dB | 13–21 dB |
| under 60 Hz, against the whole | −2.8 to −8.4 dB | −11 to −16 dB |
| 2–8 kHz / above 8 kHz | −8 to −18 / −14 to −27 dB | −20 to −26 / −27 to −43 dB |
| texture (flatness) | 0.02–0.20 | 0.0002–0.006 |
| width (side against mid) | −6.6 to −15.6 dB | varies; one was −17 |

What that turned into, on one film made against these figures and then
watched and heard (one film, one viewer):

- **Between moves the picture keeps drifting.** A move that ends at rest and
  a next one that starts from rest reads as a slideshow however good each
  move is. Give a move 9 or more frames to build: one film whose moves built
  in 5 was judged hurried.
- **The camera is `kit/camera.js` with `kit/stage.js`**, not a perspective
  transform written for the film. A hand-written one stuttered on zooms and
  showed a layer stretched 3.4× and soft; the kit moves the view’s centre and
  width together and has the browser redraw a slab at the size it is shown.
  Author every surface at the largest size it reaches.
- **The film opens on its best picture and a line about the viewer**, then
  its name. An opening that stated the scenario’s own figures (how many
  rows, how many cells) was judged impossible to follow and no reason to
  stay. The scenario is the example; the first three seconds are what the
  feature spares you.
- **The score is not a transcription of the picture.** A note for every cell
  filled, a rising run for every build and a noise sweep under every fast
  move (twelve in 37 s) were heard as wind and as machine-made. What was
  accepted: one short motif carried through the film over slow harmony, a
  phrase of four bars whose fourth bar turns round, a new phrase by the
  middle, a breath before the two big turns. A part of the film that waits
  thins the music; it does not stop it or shut a filter over it.
- **A score can sit inside every sound range and still be the wrong music.**
  Two tracks made by people were cut to the same film and both rejected. The
  ruler finds the plainly different (no low end, no top, no width, a level
  that jumps); the rest is heard, by a person.
- One very large tilted layer (a 5000 px floor) made master renders drop the
  lower part of the frame. Keep tilted layers near the frame’s size.

## What stays from before

What the earlier films rested on that the seven still support:

- **One thing followed, with a number that changes.** Five of the six carry
  one thing across most of their changes of picture — a wing from sketch to flight, a
  truck from rust to a backflip; one fails halfway with three running
  numbers, another boxes a part at 96%. The film with nothing wrong at its
  start was judged to have little in it.
- **The product's real material at the size where the trouble is real.**
- **The interface rebuilt live** — rebuilt as motion graphics, it is 14–61%
  of each of the six, and pieces of it rebuilt on a page measured back
  within a few pixels. Where their look rested on generated footage, the
  rebuilt grammar read as a diagram: bring the product's own output instead.
- **Length follows how much the product does.** The six run 41–85 s and show
  a step a shot or station, about fifteen in the longer ones; the product's
  own film that beat ours ran 57.7 s in about thirteen stretches, ours 31 s.
  A film much shorter than its product calls for should be able to say
  which functions it leaves out.
- **Using the product before planning, and drafts, sheets and a first-time
  viewer before the master** — the order below, in which the preferred films
  were made.

## The order to work in

1. **Use the product**, with something of your own put through it: every
   menu opened, each thing it does run once, the numbers and results it
   gives written down, its own words for them kept.
2. **Fill `PLAN.md` before building**: everything the product does; the
   film that comes to mind first and which marks of the tour it carries;
   then the film to make — its shape, the thing followed, its trouble and
   its number, every function in (its stretch) or out (why no viewer will
   miss it), and for each stretch its layers, what crosses its boundary,
   where the picture travels and where it holds, its words and its sound.
3. **Read one reference**, the nearer to your product: `reference/structure.md`
   §0 and §3 (three products told as one project; camera at rest while
   things are built; 57.8 s in 23 stretches), or `reference/travel/structure.md`
   §0, §3 and the film in §2 closest to yours (six request-to-result films,
   41–85 s, a new picture every 1.4–2.7 s). Its `motion.md` is for looking
   up a move as you build it. The three preferred films were made after
   reading `reference/structure.md` in full and `reference/motion.md`
   §0–4 (about 10,500 words); §0 and §3 are the least to read. Borrow
   grammar, never brand, look or names.
4. **Build the first stretch on the page**, live and acting; render a draft
   (`film render . --draft`, about half a minute), look at its sheet (`film
   sheet`) and full-size stills, then grow the film a stretch at a time.
5. **Change it.** The preferred films took 3 to 8 drafts and 2 to 4 complete
   rounds, in 64 to 77 minutes with their renders; two had a round from
   first-time viewers (`film viewer`). Start each round by checking, on the
   new draft at its second, every change the last round asked for (PLAN.md,
   "Checks"): a change written down as made is not a change seen. A stretch
   called still or slow gets the next of the product's functions happening
   there first — one the plan left out, if it comes to that; cutting is for
   what repeats (a second count, a hold on what has been read).
6. **Before the full render, full-quality stills** (`film still --at`) of
   the fastest moments and of anything much larger than the frame. A draft
   is half size with no motion blur; one film lost a 95,000 px line of text
   that way and found out after a 13-minute render.
7. **The full render** — 3 to 26 minutes for these films; start it in the
   background and wait — then look at it too: frames from the finished
   file, `film check`, `film listen`. Patch a stretch with `--from --to
   --into` rather than rendering it all again.

## What is in the folder

- `PLAN.md` — the plan to fill in step 2.
- `reference/` — the film judged a very good launch: `structure.md`
  (sections to 0.1 s, story, rhythm, sound in §6) and `motion.md` (every
  transition, curves, the camera in §4).
- `reference/travel/` — the six: `structure.md`, `motion.md` (transitions
  by kind, the camera's moves with numbers, layers, words), `sound.md`.
- `lib/` — plain functions of film time, each with a header saying what it
  is for and where its numbers come from:
  - `anim.js` — keyframes, the reference's curves, stamp, spring, fling,
    eases named by purpose and by their GSAP names, staggers, text split,
    stacked springs, anticipation, a hanging swing;
  - `moves.js` — 2.5D camera and cut moves on flat layers: a window hop, a
    pull-back and stations, a momentum cut, a one-frame jump, carries, a
    duotone developing, speed ramps and freezes; one camera handed from one
    move to the next while it is still moving;
  - `hud.js` — the words kept on screen, in the film's own accent: a status
    chip that keeps clear of the product, a counter or clock changing in
    place, a status line, a voice and its caption, a prompt typed and sent,
    cursor labels, a detection box with its count, a title out of blur;
  - `handoff.js` — a whip with one thing carried, a panel rising, a push
    through what was clicked (on `kit/seams.js`), and one thing taken
    through every place it sits over the film (`carryAlong`);
  - `devices.js` — a laptop whose lid opens and shuts, a phone, a box
    computer with a status light, the rebuilt interface live on the screen;
  - `swarm.js` — agent cursors building the product's screen box by box and
    line by line, one agent waiting visibly, flat, under a 2.5D camera or
    tilted in perspective, or over a recorded screen (`swarmBoxes`): a style
    some films use for many agents at once, not a default;
  - `space.js` and `solid.js` — a 3D stage on three.js: live windows in
    depth, a lens with depth of field and motion blur that moves by
    stations, a momentum cut or a jump as `moves.js` moves a 2D camera,
    objects made by code with studio light, and a laptop on a desk ready
    made (`S.laptop`, `S.desk`), its screen a live window;
  - `physics.js` and `physics-seeds.mjs` — rigid bodies baked once and read
    at any t, with an engine the film brings, and a script that finds a
    clean seed;
  - `measure.mjs` — a film, yours or one you admire, as a picture curve and
    a sound reading on one ruler (above, "Held beside a film you admire");
  - `palette.mjs` — the score's low bed, hits on moves, a build into a
    drop, and the six soundtracks' figures to set a score beside;
  - `sampler.mjs` with `samples/` — recorded instruments, no account needed
    (`samples/NOTICE.txt`; the piano needs a credit, `tl.credit`).
- `kit/` — `time.js` (segments, events, sources, credits) and `audio.mjs`
  (mixing, loudness, the sub and filter moves, diagnose) were used by every
  film above. Beside them `seams.js`, `stage.js` (parts of the interface as
  slabs tilted in depth, still live), `world.js` and the rest, to read, use
  or drop (`skill film kit`).
